Almost nobody can look at a garment on a hanger and know exactly how it will
read next to another one. Not for want of taste. The light is wrong, the fabric
is folded, one of the two is in another room, and colour memory is genuinely
unreliable across a distance of thirty seconds.

Professionals solve this by laying things out and looking at them. That is all
this is: looking, without having to carry both items to the same window.

{{< img src="photo-bed-with-swatches.webp" caption="Two knits, a scarf, shoes and a swatch book laid out together. Comparing them side by side is the entire method." >}}

## Why it is hard for everyone

**Light changes colour.** Two blacks match under a shop's warm lighting and
separate in daylight. That is the lighting, not your eyes.

**Hangers hide things.** A garment on a rail is folded, sideways and overlapped.
You are judging about a third of it.

**Memory is short.** The trousers are in the wardrobe, the top is in a drawer,
and by the time both are in your hands you are comparing one of them to a
recollection.

**Some people see colour differently, which is common.** Around one in twelve
men and one in two hundred women have some form of colour vision deficiency. It
is inherited and completely ordinary. If it applies to you, a rule about
undertones is no help at all, and a picture of the result is.

## Why looking beats being told

Most colour advice is vocabulary: undertone, saturation, complementary, seasonal
palette. All real, and all useless if the words do not attach to something you
can see. A picture skips the argument entirely. You do not need to know why one
of them is better in order to see that it is.
