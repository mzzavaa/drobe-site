Not fewer clothes. A narrower band of what most workplaces and most events read
as unremarkable. Skirts, dresses and heavy layering are wardrobe items like any
other, and the app treats them that way. The constraint is social, not technical,
and it is worth naming as social rather than pretending it is a fact about
bodies.

The practical effect is that when convention allows fewer silhouettes, the
difference between one outfit and another has to be carried by smaller things.

## Where it actually gets hard

**The margins are narrower.** If a shirt, trousers and a jacket are most of what
is expected, then fit, fabric and shade are doing all the work. Those are exactly
the things that are hard to judge from a hanger and easy to get slightly wrong.

**Colour, more often than anything else.** Navy against black, two greys that
turn out not to match, a brown belt with black shoes. These are hard to call
from memory for anyone, and they get harder when most of the wardrobe sits in
the same narrow range. Colour vision deficiency also runs about one in twelve
among men against one in two hundred among women, for inherited reasons. Either
way the fix is the same and it is not advice: put the two things next to each
other and look.

**Fit is most of it.** A cheap garment that fits reads better than an expensive
one that does not. Shoulder seams, sleeve length, where the trouser breaks. Hard
to judge in a changing-room mirror at an angle, straightforward when you can see
the whole body at once. Sizing between brands is no more consistent here than
anywhere else.

**Dress codes with no gradient.** Smart casual is genuinely ambiguous, and both
failure modes are live: a suit where nobody else wore one, or trainers where
they were the only pair.

**Occasion clothing bought under pressure.** A wedding in three weeks, a suit
bought in a hurry, worn once. Whether it still fits is unknown until the morning
of the next one.
